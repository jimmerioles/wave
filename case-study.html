The page is the instrument.

Wave is a browser synthesizer that doubles as its own landing page. Eight keys, five moods, zero samples. Every ring, particle and meter is drawn from the note you just played, because the page reads the live audio signal instead of a loop. The demo is the instrument.

01

The problem

A page about sound usually forgets to make any.

Instrument and audio-tool pages are demos at arm's length. A video, a screenshot, a claim about latency. The visitor is asked to imagine the product.

The pattern is familiar:

  • A video loop. Autoplay is blocked, a muted video reads like an ad, and the sound belongs to someone else's performance.
  • A fake visualizer. The bars animate on a timer. They answer to the brand, not to audio.
  • A screenshot gallery. Pixels show the interface and hide the feel.
  • A spec sheet. Numbers describe the engine without letting anyone hear it.

Wave takes the shortest route. The landing page is an actual instrument. Nothing in the demo is a recording, and nothing on the stage is decorative data.

02

What it is

A landing page you can play, not a page about one.

Eight keys span C3 to C4. Five presets reshape the oscillators, the filter, the envelope, the delay, the space and the stage's way of moving. The arp turns a held chord into a pattern, First Light is an original 12-bar cue, and the record button captures the master mix as a take.

Every sound is synthesized at runtime. The page downloads no audio files, loads no runtime libraries and talks to no backend. The instrument is static files, Web Audio and Canvas 2D.

No samples. No backend. No video loop. One page that makes the sound it advertises.

The interface behaves like a small piece of hardware. Pads depress under a pointer and hold a note when glided across. Range sliders expose their values in Hz, percent and BPM. The orb leans toward the pointer and settles on springs. The design is dark, but the controls are not mysterious.

04

How it works

Six ideas hold the whole instrument up.

The instrument is small on purpose. One voice design, one effect chain, one analyser, one canvas. No scene graph, no audio files, and no second copy of the sound.

  1. 01

    A voice is three oscillators and an envelope.

    Press A and createVoice() builds a saw, a triangle and a sine. The saw and triangle detune in opposite directions; the sine sits an octave down. A short attack and decay fall to the preset's sustain, then the release stretches when you let go. Eight voices can run at once.

  2. 02

    Every voice shares one chain.

    The dry signal joins a bus, a resonant low-pass, a 45 Hz high-pass, a soft clipper and a compressor. A ping-pong delay and a 2.6-second convolution reverb are sends. Both returns pass back through the same filter, so the filter darkens the tail as well as the note.

  3. 03

    Mute lives after the analyser.

    The analyser taps the chain before the master and the mute. Turn the sound off and the speakers go quiet, but the orb and the meters keep drawing the signal. The visualizer never performs a loop, even when the room is silent.

  4. 04

    The arp trusts the audio clock.

    A 28 ms timer looks 120 ms ahead and schedules notes against AudioContext.currentTime. Pad flashes are cosmetic and use setTimeout; the notes themselves do not depend on the page clock. Patterns are up, down, up/down and random, at 1/4, 1/8, 1/16 and 1/8T.

  5. 05

    First Light is data, not a recording.

    The cue is 48 beats of note events: start, note, length, velocity and release. It plays through the same voices as your hands, rides the filter with keyframes, and hands the filter back the moment you touch it. The arrangement is fixed; the tempo is yours.

  6. 06

    The stage is a mirror.

    A 2048-point analyser feeds the orb, the ring, the spectrum ticks and the output meter. Springs smooth level, bass and treble so motion has weight. Each preset carries its own palette and motion, and reduced motion stops the loop without stopping the instrument.

05

Performance

Nothing is decoded. Everything is drawn.

The page ships no audio files, so there is no decode step and no buffer to wait for. Sound is built from oscillators. Pixels are built from an analyser buffer.

The canvas stops when it leaves the viewport and when the tab is hidden. The easter egg halves the frame cap and schedules its music two seconds ahead, so a one-minute arrangement never creates every oscillator at once. The recorder takes 250 ms chunks and stops itself at three minutes.

The audio context asks for interactive latency, and the page waits for a gesture before it starts. These are design budgets and constants from the build, not a published benchmark. Frame pacing and audio latency still depend on the machine, the browser and the audio interface. A public trace is on the next list.

06

Accessibility

Playable by keyboard. Readable by a screen reader. Honest about sound.

The instrument is built from real controls. The canvas is the face, not the interface.

  • The pads are buttons. Each pad is a <button> with a name such as "Play C3 with the A key" and an aria-pressed state. Space and Enter play notes, and a release on blur prevents a stuck voice.
  • The keys keep their keys. Letter shortcuts do not fire while a text field or slider has focus. Range inputs keep their arrow keys and expose values as Hz, percent and BPM.
  • Toggles announce state. Presets, arp, latch, mute, record and Orb/Scope use aria-pressed; the visible label changes along with the sound.
  • Dialogs are native. About and Take are <dialog> elements with Escape, focus containment and a backdrop click to close.
  • Motion is optional. With reduced motion, the canvas stops its loop, draws a static scene and samples the meter every 150 ms. The synth still plays, and the state still updates.
  • Silence is a feature. Mute sits after the analyser, so the instrument can be used in a quiet room without losing the visual response.
  • This page follows the same rules. One heading, semantic landmarks, a skip link, visible focus, table captions and alt text. No meaning depends on color alone.

The honest limit: the instrument is auditory. The visualizer is aria-hidden, and there is no text or haptic equivalent for pitch. A screen reader can operate every control and read every state, but it cannot hear the result for the user. That belongs in the limitations, not in a claim.

07

Limitations

What this does not claim.

A prototype is more useful when it names its edges.

  • The keybed is one octave, C3 to C4. First Light uses D4 and E4 above it; the pads stop at C4.
  • The arp is monophonic per step. There is no swing, no per-step gate and no 1/32 rate.
  • First Light is a fixed 12-bar cue. Tempo changes; the arrangement does not.
  • Recording is session-only MediaRecorder output, capped at three minutes. There is no WAV export and no sharing. Some browsers omit duration metadata until playback.
  • MIDI covers note on, note off and velocity. CC, pitch bend and clock are ignored.
  • Audio needs a user gesture and Web Audio support. The page is silent until that gesture, by design.
  • The visualizer is an enhancement and is hidden from assistive tech. The instrument is auditory; there is no text or haptic equivalent for pitch.
  • Frame pacing and latency depend on the device. There is no published benchmark yet.
  • The easter egg is a short synthesized quote of a copyrighted song, credited in About and the README. It is an egg, not a feature.
08

What is next

The next version should make the instrument more playable, not bigger.

The demo has proved the argument. The remaining work is evidence, reach and range.

  1. 01

    Publish a frame and latency trace.

    Measure pan, animation and input latency on a mid-range laptop and a phone, then put the numbers beside the budgets.

  2. 02

    Add a second octave.

    An octave shift or a 16-pad mode, without losing the one-octave clarity.

  3. 03

    Give the arp a feel.

    Swing, per-step gates and a 1/32 rate. The clock is already sample-accurate; the pattern needs a human lean.

  4. 04

    Export a take as WAV.

    Render offline for a lossless file, then add a share link so a take can leave the tab.

  5. 05

    Teach MIDI more.

    Map CC to filter, echo and space. Accept clock and pitch bend.

  6. 06

    Cut a 60-second explainer.

    Use the shot list above, then put the short cut at the top of the README.

  7. 07

    Build a second instrument.

    Reuse the engine and the stage to prove the pattern is a product, not a one-off.
